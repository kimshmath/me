1. Introduction

In “Catching Crumbs from the Table,” a short story published in Nature in 2000, Ted Chiang imagined a world in which “metahumans,” beings whose intelligence far surpasses that of humans, have taken over scientific research entirely, while human scientists have retreated into the role of interpreting their results after the fact. The story takes the form of a journal editorial written twenty-five years after the last original research paper by a human author was submitted to the journal. The metahumans exchange their findings through a medium of their own that is inaccessible to humans, and human journals have become venues for explicating those findings in human language. The question the story raises—what role is left for human researchers once the frontier of inquiry has moved beyond the reach of human understanding?—was a thought experiment when the story appeared. Since 2024, however, the achievements of artificial intelligence (AI) in mathematics have turned it into a real-world question.

The purpose of this essay is to survey the debate surrounding the question “Can machines think?” with a focus on the case of mathematics. Mathematics is a fitting focus for two reasons. First, it was in mathematics, through the analysis of computation and proof, that the concept of “mechanical thinking” was first rigorously defined. Second, because a mathematical claim stands or falls not on experimental apparatus or data but on the validity of its argument, mathematics has served as a touchstone for gauging the reasoning abilities of AI. Rather than advancing a new argument, the essay seeks to organize the relevant discussions and to identify where the issues lie.

The discussion turns on three questions. The first is the question Turing posed in 1950: “Can machines think?” The second is the question Wittgenstein raised in his remarks on the foundations of mathematics: “Does a calculating machine calculate?” Whereas the first is directed at the capacities of machines, the second asks under what conditions the exercise of those capacities counts as calculation or as thought. The third question follows from the other two: in an age when machines solve problems, what should human beings take as the task of their thinking?

The essay is organized as follows. Section 2 traces the conceptual history of mechanical thinking from Leibniz to Turing and Gödel. Sections 3 and 4 examine Turing’s conception of the learning machine and the lineage through which that conception came to be realized in large language models. Section 5 surveys, in chronological order, the changes that took place in mathematics between 2024 and 2026, and Section 6 examines the problems of proof, verification, and understanding that these changes have raised, in light of the controversy over the four-color theorem and the views of Wittgenstein and Thurston. I should state at the outset that many of the results discussed in Section 5 have yet to be fully verified as of November 2026, and that I took part as a researcher in some of them.

2. A Conceptual History of Mechanical Thinking: From Leibniz to Gödel

The idea of reducing thought to calculation takes definite shape with Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716). In a number of drafts from the late 1670s, he envisaged a universal characteristic (characteristica universalis), which would assign a distinctive number or symbol to every concept, together with a calculus of reasoning (calculus ratiocinator) by which those symbols would be manipulated according to rules. He expected that, once this plan was realized, philosophical disputes would give way to the checking of calculations, and that whenever opinions differed it would suffice to say, “Let us calculate” (calculemus). In his “Preface to a Universal Characteristic” (c. 1678–79), he anticipated that this instrument would enlarge the powers of the mind even more than optical lenses had aided the eye. His design of a calculating machine capable of the four arithmetic operations, a model of which he presented to the Royal Society in London in 1673, and his invention of binary arithmetic around 1679 were of a piece with the same vision. His machine, however, never worked reliably because of a defect in its carrying mechanism, and the universal characteristic never progressed beyond the stage of a plan. The comparison of scientific instruments to lenses recurs to this day: in an essay published in the journal Daedalus in 2026, Pushmeet Kohli of Google DeepMind characterized AI as a “revolutionary lens” for deciphering the nature of reality.

What Leibniz’s project left undefined was the very concept of calculation. This gap was filled in response to the decision problem (Entscheidungsproblem) that David Hilbert (1862–1943) posed in the 1920s: the question whether there exists a general method for deciding, by a finite procedure, whether a given mathematical statement is provable. In his 1936 paper “On Computable Numbers,” Alan Turing (1912–1954) analyzed the activity of a person calculating according to fixed rules into elementary operations that cannot be decomposed any further. The abstract device that resulted, the Turing machine, does nothing more than repeat, in accordance with a finite table of rules, the operations of reading a single symbol on a tape divided into squares, rewriting it, and moving one square to the left or right. The claim that every calculation a person can carry out by following a mechanical procedure can be carried out by a Turing machine is known as the Church–Turing thesis. The thesis is not a theorem that could be proved but a proposed definition of the intuitive notion of “computability,” and its persuasiveness was reinforced by the fact that it is coextensive with the definition that Alonzo Church had put forward independently in the same year. Writing in 1964, Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) likewise judged Turing’s analysis to be the definitive definition of the concept of a mechanical procedure. Turing also constructed a universal machine, which takes as its input the rule table of an arbitrary Turing machine and simulates that machine’s operation; it is regarded as the theoretical prototype of the stored-program computer. From the standpoint of computability, today’s digital computers, and the AI that runs on them, all fall within the scope of the Turing machine.

The definition of computation led directly to a proof of its limits. In the same paper, Turing showed that there can be no Turing machine that decides whether an arbitrary Turing machine will finish its computation and halt—the result known today as the undecidability of the halting problem. Simple rules do not make it easy to tell whether a machine will halt. Consider a machine that repeatedly divides a number by 2 if it is even, and multiplies it by 3 and adds 1 if it is odd: whether it reaches 1 and halts for every positive integer has remained unresolved since the question was raised in the 1930s (the Collatz conjecture). The undecidability of the halting problem settled the decision problem in the negative. Since a proof is a procedure that starts from axioms and applies rules of inference one step at a time—the very paradigm of mechanical thinking—this result means that mechanical thinking alone cannot resolve every mathematical problem. It is an impossibility theorem of the same family as the incompleteness theorems that Gödel proved in 1931. According to the incompleteness theorems, if a formal system whose axioms can be mechanically enumerated and which contains arithmetic is consistent, then there are statements in it that can be neither proved nor refuted, and the system cannot prove its own consistency. Hilbert closed an address he gave in Königsberg in September 1930 with the words “We must know. We will know,” declaring his conviction that there are no unsolvable problems in mathematics; yet it was at a conference held in the same city on the very day before that Gödel had first announced this theorem to the scholarly world, ahead of its publication in a paper. The theorems of Gödel and Turing showed that, as far as formal procedures are concerned, this conviction cannot be sustained.

In his Gibbs Lecture of 1951, Gödel formulated the philosophical implications of the incompleteness theorems as a single claim: either the human mind surpasses the powers of any finite machine, or else there exist mathematical problems that are absolutely unsolvable by human beings. Put briefly, either humans are greater than machines, or mathematics is greater than humans. Gödel himself leaned toward the first alternative. Later, J. R. Lucas, in a 1961 paper, and Roger Penrose, in his 1989 book The Emperor’s New Mind, sought to derive directly from the incompleteness theorems the conclusion that the human mind is not a machine. Their arguments, however, have been criticized for presupposing the consistency and infallibility of human mathematicians, and the prevailing assessment today is that Gödel’s claim itself stands, but that the theorems alone do not decide which of its alternatives is true.

What should be noted here is that all of these impossibility theorems presuppose a machine that follows its rules without error. Yet the actual research of mathematicians is not an accumulation of formal derivations; it relies heavily on analogy, intuition, and trial and error, and a formal proof is closer to an after-the-fact systematization of its results. The question therefore shifts to whether a machine can adopt such informal modes of thinking—in other words, whether a machine can go beyond mechanical thinking.

3. Machines Permitted to Err: Turing’s Conception of the Learning Machine

The first systematic answer to this question came from none other than Turing, who had himself proved one of these impossibility theorems. In a 1947 lecture to the London Mathematical Society, he pointed out that if a machine is expected to be infallible, it cannot also be expected to be intelligent, and that the impossibility theorems say nothing about how much intelligence a machine might display if it makes no pretense of infallibility. The way out of the impossibility theorems, in other words, is to permit the machine to err.

Turing developed this idea in his 1950 paper “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” and in the text of a lecture from around 1951, “Intelligent Machinery, A Heretical Theory.” In the latter, Turing announces that his purpose is to question the commonplace that you cannot make a machine to think for you, and he proposes in its place a machine that modifies itself in the light of experience. Such a machine will at times make false statements, but it may also produce new statements that its designer did not foresee. The proposal in the 1950 paper—that instead of writing a program for an adult intelligence directly, we should build a machine corresponding to a child and educate it—belongs to the same line of thought. The “unorganised machine” that he conceived in his 1948 report “Intelligent Machinery,” a network of simple, randomly connected elements that becomes organized through training, is regarded as a forerunner of artificial neural networks. Turing went further, predicting that once such machines began to think, it would not be long before they outstripped human powers, and that the machines would converse with one another to sharpen their wits.

“Computing Machinery and Intelligence” examines in turn nine objections that might be raised against machine intelligence, two of which bear directly on the theme of this essay. One is what Turing called “the Mathematical Objection”: the claim that the theorems of Gödel and of Turing himself set insurmountable limits on what machines can do. Turing conceded that every particular machine has its limitations, but replied that the claim that no such limitations apply to the human intellect had merely been asserted, without any proof. Later criticisms of the arguments of Lucas and Penrose are an extension of this reply. The other objection derives from a view of Ada Lovelace (1815–1852). In the notes she appended in 1843 to an exposition of Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine, Lovelace wrote that the Engine can do only what we know how to order it to perform and cannot originate anything of itself. Turing replied that machines take him by surprise with great frequency, and he held that the objection does not apply to machines that learn. Today’s question of whether machines can produce new mathematical results may be regarded as Lovelace’s objection raised anew.

In short, Turing distinguished between the infallible machine that follows rules and the fallible machine that learns and makes mistakes, and he located the possibility of intelligence in the latter. From the standpoint of computability, both are Turing machines; but the operation of the former resembles deduction, while that of the latter resembles induction and trial and error. If the mainstream of AI research in the second half of the twentieth century followed the first path—the automation of symbol manipulation and logical inference—research since the 2010s has moved to the second: learning from large-scale data.

The first path took mathematics as its proving ground from the outset. The Logic Theorist, a program created in 1956 by Allen Newell, Herbert Simon, and their collaborators, proved 38 of the first 52 theorems in Chapter 2 of Whitehead and Russell’s Principia Mathematica, and in a 1958 article the two predicted that within ten years a computer would discover and prove an important new mathematical theorem. Achievements along this path that could be called solutions of open problems, however, were rare. The example usually cited is the proof in 1996, by William McCune’s automated theorem prover, of the Robbins conjecture in Boolean algebra, which had been posed in the 1930s. Results matching Simon and Newell’s prediction began to appear one after another only some sixty years later—and they came by way of learning machines.

4. The Lineage of Large Language Models

A large language model is a statistical model trained to predict the word that comes next in a given context. Its prototype can be found in “A Mathematical Theory of Communication,” the 1948 paper by Claude Shannon (1916–2001). Treating language as a stochastic process, Shannon generated English sentences by choosing each next word at random according to probabilities that depended only on the single preceding word. The sentences obtained in this way made no sense, but locally they had the syntactic structure of English. In 1954 the linguist Zellig Harris put forward the distributional hypothesis, according to which the meaning of a word is characterized by the distribution of the contexts in which it occurs. The idea is often compared with the view of the later Wittgenstein, who located the meaning of a word in its use.

In the 2010s, these two ideas were combined with neural network techniques. The word-embedding method published by Tomas Mikolov and colleagues in 2013 represents words as vectors with several hundred dimensions, and after training on large quantities of text it was found that semantic relations between words appear as geometric relations between their vectors. For example, the difference between the vectors for “Paris” and “France” is approximately equal to the difference between those for “Seoul” and “Korea.” The Transformer architecture proposed by Ashish Vaswani and colleagues in 2017 introduced an attention mechanism, through which the words of a sentence refer to one another and thereby acquire representations suited to their context. This makes it possible to distinguish meanings that vary with context, such as the three senses of “fair” in “The weather was fair on the day of the fair, but the judging was hardly fair.” Inasmuch as it predicts the next word conditioned not on the immediately preceding word but on the entire context, a large language model can be understood as an extension of Shannon’s model.

Viewed in the light of Leibniz’s project of assigning numbers to concepts and thereby turning reasoning into calculation, large language models may be said to have realized that project in an unexpected way. The differences, however, are also clear. Leibniz’s symbols were to be assigned in advance, through the analysis of concepts, whereas the numerical representations of a language model are learned after the fact from the statistics of language use. Moreover, the output of a language model carries no deductive guarantee, and it is structurally accompanied by so-called hallucination: the generation of plausible-sounding content that is contrary to fact. In this respect, language models are precisely the fallible machines that Turing spoke of.

Whether such models can be said to “understand” language is a matter of debate. Rather than intervening directly in that debate, this essay will only observe that the task of predicting the next word is by no means trivial. For to predict the next step of a proof correctly, the mathematical content with which the proof deals must be present in the model in some form. Mathematics is a domain well suited to testing this point.

5. Developments in Mathematics, 2024–2026

There is a wide gap between a plausible sentence and a valid proof, for a single faulty step invalidates the whole of a proof. The International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO) has served as a yardstick for measuring this gap. In 2024, Google DeepMind’s AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry 2 solved four of the six problems and scored 28 points, at the level of a silver medal; but the translation of the problems into the formal language Lean was done by humans, and the solutions took up to three days. A proof assistant such as Lean mechanically checks whether each step of a proof written in a formal language conforms to the rules of inference. The arrangement in which a trained model generates proofs and a proof assistant checks them amounts to a division of labor between the two kinds of machine distinguished in Section 3: the output of the fallible machine is screened by the infallible one. In 2025, Gemini Deep Think, a language model from the same company, worked from problems stated in natural language and solved five of them within the official competition time of four and a half hours, scoring 35 points, at the level of a gold medal.

Olympiad problems differ from research-level mathematics in that their setters know the answers. In 2026, attention shifted to open problems. Aletheia, unveiled by Google DeepMind in early 2026, is a language-model-based mathematical research agent that cycles through generating, verifying, and revising solutions. It is designed to filter out errors by having the solutions it generates reviewed in a separate stage. The research team (Feng et al.) applied the system to 700 open problems left by Paul Erdős (1913–1996). According to their 2026 paper, “Towards Autonomous Mathematics Research,” of the 200 responses that were graded, 137 (68.5%) contained fundamental errors, while 13 (6.5%) were correct in the sense the problem intended. A tendency to interpret problems in the easiest possible way, producing answers that are correct only in a formal sense, was also reported. Of these 13, four were new answers to questions that had until then been open, and two of them were classified as autonomous solutions of the problem as a whole. One of the two, Problem 1051—a 1980 problem on the irrationality of rapidly converging series—subsequently went through questions raised by human researchers, a generalization proposed by the AI, and the completion of a rigorous proof by humans, and was written up as a joint paper. This result, obtained by a research team that included me, is forthcoming in the Bulletin of the London Mathematical Society. The case illustrates one route by which an AI’s solution, once understood and extended by humans, enters the mathematical literature.

The First Proof experiment of February 2026 was designed to rule out the possibility that answers had leaked through training data. Eleven mathematicians made public ten problems that had arisen in the course of their own research but had not yet been published, and allowed about a week for solutions. Aletheia produced its answers without human intervention, and in the assessment of a majority of experts its answers to six of the problems (Problems 2, 5, 7, 8, 9, and 10) were correct. OpenAI stated that at least five of its own model’s answers were likely to be correct. Problem 7 deserves particular attention. It is a problem in topology that its setter, Shmuel Weinberger, had presented as an open problem in a 2023 book, and Aletheia’s answer reaches its conclusion by combining major theorems and theories of twentieth-century mathematics that bear the names of Selberg, Cartan, Leray, Borel, Atiyah, Novikov, Kasparov, and others. In building its argument by invoking highly compressed existing theories rather than accumulating individual computations, the answer resembles the way a researcher experienced in the field would solve the problem. Yet in their provisional assessment before the deadline, the Aletheia team judged this answer to be probably wrong, and they reclassified it as correct only after further review. This was because the answer had been produced shortly before the deadline, and checking whether those deep theories had been validly applied took a long time. That even the team that built the system could not immediately determine the validity of its output foreshadows the problem of verification discussed below.

From May 2026 onward, industrial research laboratories and the researchers affiliated with them announced solutions to well-known open problems in quick succession. Table 1 summarizes the principal cases, as announced by the respective institutions and researchers.

The problems listed in the table differ from one another in field and character. The unit distance problem is a question in combinatorial geometry that asks for the largest possible number of pairs at distance exactly 1 among n points in the plane. In 1946 Erdős conjectured that arrangements in the shape of a square grid are essentially optimal, and that the number of such pairs can never rise much above a level proportional to n. OpenAI’s model disproved this conjecture by constructing arrangements in which the number of such pairs exceeds n raised to some fixed exponent greater than 1, and Will Sawin of Princeton University soon afterward made the exponent explicit as 1.014. The Jacobian conjecture, posed by Ott-Heinrich Keller in 1939, asserts that a polynomial map whose Jacobian determinant is a nonzero constant has an inverse that is also given by polynomials. The counterexample presented by Anthropic shows that the conjecture is false when there are three or more variables; the two-variable case remains open. A Hadamard matrix is a square matrix with entries 1 and −1 in which any two distinct rows are orthogonal. It has been conjectured since 1893 that such a matrix exists in every order that is a multiple of 4, and since 2005 the order 668 had been the smallest for which existence had not been confirmed. The Navier–Stokes problem is a problem in analysis that asks whether the equations describing the motion of a three-dimensional fluid always have smooth solutions. Spanning combinatorial geometry, algebraic geometry, combinatorics, and analysis, this list shows that AI’s achievements are not confined to any particular field.

Of these, the disproof of the unit distance conjecture was accepted after review by outside mathematicians, and the counterexample to the Jacobian conjecture, which consists of a single polynomial map in three variables, could be checked by anyone. By contrast, OpenAI’s solution of the Navier–Stokes problem, one of the Millennium Prize Problems, was made public as a 166-page paper together with a formal proof written in Lean, and as of November 2026 it is still under review by the mathematical community. The release of October 6 differs from earlier announcements in its scale. OpenAI presented some 4,000 problems to a model that had not yet been released and published the results as 722 papers. Counted with related papers grouped together, these amount to 372 clusters of results, among them results on some of the best-known hard problems in their respective fields: the Cannon conjecture, the non-amenability of Thompson’s group F, a counterexample to Kaplansky’s zero-divisor conjecture, the free group factor problem, and the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, a weak form of the Riemann hypothesis.

The reaction of the mathematical community has been divided between astonishment and concern. In a piece written in September 2026, Daniel Litt of the University of Toronto observed that AI, which three years earlier could not be trusted to add two numbers, had reached Olympiad gold-medal level a year earlier and was now solving major open problems autonomously, and he argued that the profession of mathematician must be fundamentally reconsidered. Writing in October of the same year about the proof of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, Frank Calegari, a number theorist at the University of Chicago, remarked that he would sooner have expected a proof of the Riemann hypothesis itself. This was because the proof had been obtained by extending an approach that had been considered to have no chance of success.

Not all of the released results were correct, however. Of the papers released on October 6, three were withdrawn within a day because of sign errors, and fourteen were revised. A more fundamental problem is the burden of verification. Reading one paper a day, a single person would need two years to get through all 722, and only some of them are accompanied by formal verification. Problems remain even where formal verification has been carried out. Because the proof for the Navier–Stokes problem has passed formal verification, few have questioned its correctness. Yet to my knowledge, no one has so far reported gaining any new insight into the behavior of fluids from this proof. I would sum up this situation as “a flood of discovery, a drought of verification, and the disappearance of understanding.” By this I mean that results are being produced faster than they can be verified, and that even results that have been verified come without an understanding of why they are true.

These concerns were given formal expression on September 11, 2026, in a statement signed by twenty-five Fields Medalists, “A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics.” The statement diagnoses a misalignment between the goals of the AI industry and those of the mathematical community. The purpose of mathematics, it holds, lies in the advancement of understanding, and the solution of famous problems is both a means to that end and a measure of it; when the rapid production of solutions usurps the place of the end, understanding may be undermined. While acknowledging AI’s potential to advance mathematical research, the statement notes that the same problem is arising in other disciplines and in creative fields, and urges the mathematical community, AI developers, and society as a whole to respond urgently.

6. At Issue: Proof, Verification, and Understanding

The question of whether a proof produced by a machine can be accepted as mathematical knowledge has precedents. In proving the four-color theorem in 1976, Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken left the checking of nearly two thousand cases to a computer. In a 1979 paper, the philosopher Thomas Tymoczko argued that a proof which no human can follow and check from beginning to end—a proof that lacks surveyability—differs from a proof in the traditional sense, and that to accept such a proof is to introduce an empirical element into mathematics. In a 1980 paper, Paul Teller countered that surveyability is not a constitutive element of the concept of proof. In his 1994 paper “On Proof and Progress in Mathematics,” William Thurston (1946–2012) interpreted the controversy as reflecting not so much doubts about the truth of the theorem or the correctness of the proof as a desire for human understanding of the proof. In 2005, Georges Gonthier used a proof assistant to formally verify the entire proof of the four-color theorem. This settled the doubts about the reliability of the proof, but a conceptual explanation of why four colors suffice has still not been given. This precedent already shows that verification and understanding are separate matters.

Cases in which the burden of verification exceeded the capacities of referees recurred thereafter. When Thomas Hales announced in 1998 a proof of the Kepler conjecture (1611) on the densest way of packing spheres of equal size, the proof depended on extensive computer calculations. After four years of scrutiny, the panel of twelve referees appointed by the Annals of Mathematics concluded that they were 99 percent certain the proof was correct but could not certify the correctness of all of the computer calculations. Hales then embarked on a project to verify the entire proof with proof assistants and completed it in 2014. Formal verification thus became established as a means of securing the correctness of proofs that human review cannot handle. What formal verification added, however, was certainty about the proof; the question of understanding the proof remained, as in the case of the four-color theorem, a separate task.

Both precedents concerned a single theorem, and the task left to the machine was the checking of finitely many cases. The situation in 2026 differs from them in two respects: it involves hundreds of results, and the task assigned to the machine is not checking but the conception and construction of the proofs themselves. How automation would change the values and practices of mathematical research had been discussed among mathematicians before 2026 as well, notably in Akshay Venkatesh’s 2024 essay, but those discussions concerned an anticipated future.

Turing’s 1950 paper proposed replacing the question “Can machines think?” with a criterion—whether a machine can be distinguished from a human being in a conversation conducted in writing—that is, with the imitation game. By this criterion, whether thinking is taking place is judged by output, and today’s language models appear to meet the criterion to a considerable extent. In the same paper Turing predicted that in about fifty years an average interrogator would have no more than a 70 percent chance of making the right identification after five minutes of questioning. In an experiment reported by Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen in 2025, interrogators who had held five-minute text conversations judged the language model, rather than the human participant, to be the human 73 percent of the time in the case of the best-performing model. Yet there were dissenting views early on as to whether output alone suffices. Turing attended the lectures on the foundations of mathematics that Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951) gave at Cambridge in 1939, and the two argued over such questions as how a contradiction within a mathematical system affects its applications. In a manuscript of 1947, Wittgenstein wrote of Turing’s machines, “These machines are humans who calculate,” and in Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics, published posthumously in 1956, he asks, “Does a calculating machine calculate?”

His point is made by way of two imaginary situations. In the first, a calculating machine that has come into existence by accident has its buttons pressed by accident, and it displays the product of 25 and 20. In the second, calculating machines exist as natural objects in a society whose members cannot open them to look inside, and people use them to make predictions. According to Wittgenstein, for the people in the second situation, operating the machine is not calculating but experimenting. A process becomes a calculation not because its result is accurate, but because it is placed within a context in which the signs are also used in practices outside mathematics and results are justified by reference to rules. His demand that a mathematical proof be perspicuous (übersichtlich) can be read in the same spirit. Seen from this perspective, a proof produced by a model whose inner workings cannot be inspected, and whose reasoning humans cannot follow, has not yet acquired the status of a proof within human mathematical practice, even if it is correct. If Turing’s question is directed at the capacities of machines, Wittgenstein’s is directed at the conditions under which their outputs acquire meaning; and the situation in 2026 raises the latter question anew, in the form “Are machines thinking?”

The view that the purpose of mathematics lies in understanding received its clearest expression from Thurston. In the 1994 paper mentioned above, he argued that what we should ask is not how mathematicians prove theorems but how mathematicians advance human understanding of mathematics. This claim was grounded in his own experience. In foliation theory, which he had studied since his days as a graduate student, Thurston proved a series of major theorems within a short time; within the next couple of years, researchers began to leave the field. He traced the cause not to an exhaustion of problems but to two things: his results had been written up in a form that was hard to follow for anyone who did not share his background, and what researchers wanted was not the answers themselves but to understand for themselves. In his subsequent work on the geometrization of three-manifolds, he recalls, he did not hurry to publish proofs but put his effort into sharing his ideas by circulating lecture notes and giving talks, with the result that many researchers came to take part in the field. His observation that a field can actually wither when the production of results outpaces the sharing of understanding anticipates what the “disappearance of understanding” described above may lead to.

In 2010, an anonymous question was posted on MathOverflow, a question-and-answer site for mathematicians: what can an ordinary person, not a genius like Gauss or Euler, contribute to mathematics? In his answer, Thurston wrote that the product of mathematics is not theorems in themselves but “clarity and understanding,” and that mathematics exists only in the activity of a living community of mathematicians. He added that mathematics with the people taken out of it would be more like computer code.

The distance that the anonymous questioner felt from such geniuses is structurally the same as the distance that mathematicians in 2026 feel from AI. If Thurston’s answer is right, the fact that machines surpass humans in problem-solving ability does not do away with the human role in mathematics. The Fields Medalists’ statement, in defining the solution of problems as a means to understanding, can be seen as a reaffirmation of this position. From this standpoint, the work of reading machine-produced proofs, picking out their key ideas, connecting them with existing theory, and rewriting them in a form that others can understand lies not at the periphery of mathematical research but at its center. Erdős Problem 1051 and First Proof Problem 7, discussed above, are respectively a case in which this work was carried out and a case in which it was delayed. Formal verification cannot do this work in our stead, for what formal verification guarantees is the truth of a proposition, not a human grasp of why the proposition is true.

7. Concluding Remarks

This essay has surveyed the path by which the concept of mechanical thinking passed from Leibniz’s project to Turing’s definition, and then, by way of Turing’s conception of the learning machine, to large language models; it has also reviewed the achievements and issues that have emerged in mathematics since 2024. The discussion may be summarized as follows. First, the impossibility theorems of Turing and Gödel concern infallible formal procedures and do not limit the capacities of machines that are permitted to err and that learn. Second, as of 2026 such machines have reached the stage of solving research-level mathematical problems, and by the criterion of output, the question “Can machines think?” is being answered in the affirmative. Third, what has come to the fore as a result is the problem of verification and understanding, and this problem calls for a fresh examination of Wittgenstein’s question “Does a calculating machine calculate?” and of Thurston’s position that the purpose of mathematics lies in understanding.

What remains is the third question: what, then, should we be thinking about? Ted Chiang’s story ends with the assertion that we need not be intimidated by the achievements of the metahumans, and it gives as its reason that the technologies that made the metahumans possible were originally human inventions. Indeed, Turing’s 1936 paper and Gödel’s theorems, which became the theoretical foundations of today’s AI, were attempts to answer questions in the foundations of mathematics; they were not research undertaken for practical ends. As Abraham Flexner pointed out in his 1939 essay “The Usefulness of Useless Knowledge,” most of the discoveries that have brought great benefit to humankind arose not from the pursuit of usefulness but from the desire to satisfy curiosity. The Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, of which Flexner was the founding director, was established on the strength of this conviction; it is also where Gödel spent most of his research life and where John von Neumann built a stored-program computer. When the solving of problems can be delegated to machines, what remains that cannot be delegated is the posing of questions and the understanding of their answers.

The main point of this essay applies to almost every field of learning that humankind has built up, for reading texts, weighing their validity, interpreting their meaning, and sharing it within a community is the work that all scholarship has in common. What is required of mathematicians in the face of machine-produced mathematics is simply no different. In Ted Chiang’s story, the retreat of human scientists into the role of interpreters is portrayed as a fall in status; but from the standpoint that takes understanding to be the aim of scholarship, interpretation and communication have always belonged to the very core of scholarly work. The aims of education, too, will shift their center from the acquisition of correct answers to the formulation of questions and the experience of understanding.

About the picture at the top

How many pairs among n points in the plane can be at distance exactly 1? In 1946 Erdős conjectured that a square grid is essentially optimal, and in May 2026 a machine disproved it (Section 5). This picture follows that construction on a small scale: points of a lattice in eight dimensions are projected onto the plane, and every two at distance exactly 1 are joined. Turning the direction of projection scatters the lengths; in each special direction they all return to 1. The lattices in the actual disproof have far higher dimension. Drag the figure, or press the tilt button, to see the lattice projected into space from any side.
